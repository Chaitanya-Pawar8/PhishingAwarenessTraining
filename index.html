Security Awareness Module — Task 2

You just got an email asking you to act fast. Is it real?

Phishing doesn't look like a hacker in a hoodie — it looks like your bank, your IT department, or your boss. This module walks through how these messages are built to deceive you, how to catch them in seconds, and how to prove it to yourself with a short scored check at the end.

Exhibit A

The anatomy of a phishing email

This is a fictional example built to show the pattern — not a real company. Click each underlined phrase to see why it's a red flag.

From
To you@yourcompany.com

We detected unusual sign-in activity on your account. To avoid a suspension of services, you must within 24 hours. Failure to do so will result in permanent account closure.

Thank you for you're continued business with us.

6 red flags are hidden in this message.

1

Mismatched sender domain

The display name says "Northwind Bank," but the actual address is a lookalike domain the bank has never used. Always check the part after the @ symbol, not the friendly name.

2

Manufactured urgency

A tight deadline and a threat of loss (suspension, closure, fees) is designed to make you act before you think. Legitimate institutions rarely give you 24 hours to avoid account closure by email.

3

Generic greeting

A real bank that holds your account already knows your name. "Valued Customer" usually means the sender doesn't actually have your details — they mass-sent this to thousands of addresses.

4

Request to "verify" credentials

Legitimate services almost never ask you to confirm a password or full account number via an emailed link. This is the actual goal of the message — harvesting your login.

5

Button hides the real link

The button text says "Verify My Account," but the underlying link (visible by hovering, not shown here for safety) usually points to an unrelated domain. Never trust what a button says — trust where it actually goes.

6

Spelling & grammar slips

"You're" instead of "your," odd phrasing, and inconsistent formatting are common in mass phishing campaigns — though attackers using AI writing tools are closing this gap, so don't rely on it alone.

Exhibit B

Fake websites: what the address bar tells you

Phishing emails usually lead to a cloned login page. The page can look pixel-perfect — the address bar is where the deception breaks down.

🔒
!

The real domain is the part right before the first single slash

"northwindbank" here is just a subdomain of login-secure-id.com — an attacker-owned domain. The bank's actual site is northwindbank.com, with nothing extra attached.

Check the padlock, but don't trust it alone. HTTPS only means the connection is encrypted — attackers can and do buy valid certificates for fake domains.

Read the domain right-to-left from the first slash. The true domain is always immediately before the first single "/" — everything before that, separated by dots, is a subdomain.

Watch for character swaps. "rn" instead of "m," a zero instead of "o," or an extra hyphen are common lookalike tricks (e.g. paypa1.com, arnazon.com).

Type the address yourself instead of clicking. When in doubt, open a new tab and navigate to the site directly rather than following a link from an email or text.

Be suspicious of urgency plus a login form. That combination — "act now" and "sign in here" — is the single most common phishing pattern on the web.

Check for a working "forgot password" and support page. Cloned sites are often single-page — real account/support infrastructure is expensive to fake in full.

Exhibit C

The social engineering playbook

Phishing is one delivery method for a much older set of manipulation tactics. Attackers mix and match these to bypass your instincts, not your firewall.

Urgency & fear

Manufactured deadlines, threats of loss, or claims of a security breach short-circuit careful thinking.

"Your account will be locked in 1 hour."

Authority

Impersonating an executive, IT admin, or government agency to make a request feel non-optional.

"This is the CEO — I need this handled discreetly."

Curiosity & baiting

An enticing subject line, attachment, or "prize" gets a target to click before thinking it through.

"Invoice attached — see item you were charged for."

Familiarity

Referencing a real coworker, project, or recent event to appear like a legitimate, ongoing conversation.

"Following up on the file I sent Tuesday."

Scarcity

Limited-time offers or "only 3 spots left" pressure people into skipping their normal verification steps.

"Offer expires in 15 minutes."

Pretexting

A fabricated scenario — an IT ticket, an audit, a delivery issue — used to justify an unusual request for information.

"I'm from IT, confirming your login for the migration."

Vishing & smishing

The same tactics delivered by phone call or text message, often used to add believability to an email that came first.

A call "confirming" the email you just received.

Quid pro quo

Offering a service or favor — tech support, a gift card, a refund — in exchange for information or access.

"I can fix that for you, just confirm your ID first."

Tailgating

The physical-world version: following an employee through a secure door using politeness and social pressure instead of a badge.

"Can you hold the door? I forgot my badge."
Exhibit D

Real-world case files

Phishing isn't a hypothetical. These are among the most cited incidents in security training, in chronological order.

2011

RSA SecurID breach

Attackers sent a small group of employees a spear-phishing email titled "2011 Recruitment Plan" with a malicious spreadsheet attached. Opening it exploited a flaw in Flash, giving attackers a foothold that ultimately compromised data related to RSA's SecurID two-factor authentication products.

2013

Target customer data breach

Attackers phished login credentials from a third-party HVAC vendor with network access to Target's systems, then pivoted into point-of-sale systems — exposing tens of millions of customers' payment card details. It remains a textbook example of supply-chain risk starting with a single phishing email.

2013–2015

The $100M+ Google & Facebook invoice scam

Lithuanian national Evaldas Rimasauskas ran a business email compromise scheme, sending fake invoices that impersonated a real hardware vendor both companies worked with. Employees wired well over $100 million combined to attacker-controlled accounts before it was caught.

2020

Twitter employee vishing attack

Attackers called Twitter employees posing as IT support to obtain internal credentials, eventually gaining access to account-management tools. High-profile accounts were used to post a cryptocurrency scam — a case where a phone call (vishing), not an email, was the entry point.

Exhibit E

Your defense checklist

None of this requires special tools — just a short pause and a habit of verifying before you act.

Pause on urgency. If a message is pushing you to act immediately, that pressure is itself a warning sign — take the extra thirty seconds.

Verify through a second channel. Got an odd request from your "boss" or "bank"? Call them using a number you already have on file — never one from the message.

Hover before you click. On desktop, hovering over a link shows the real destination in the status bar. On mobile, press and hold instead of tapping.

Never enter credentials from an email link. Navigate to the site yourself, or use a saved bookmark, instead of following a link to a login page.

Treat unexpected attachments with suspicion. Especially invoices, shipping notices, or "resumes" you weren't expecting — confirm with the sender first.

Use multi-factor authentication everywhere it's offered. It won't stop every attack, but it turns a stolen password into a dead end most of the time.

Report, don't just delete. Forward suspicious emails to your IT/security team — one report can protect the rest of your organization.

Keep software and browsers updated. Many phishing pages rely on exploiting outdated software once you land on them.
